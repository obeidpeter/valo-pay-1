This records why the console is the way it is: the landing and sign-in pages first, then each state and each audit in the sections below, so the next change is made against the same reasoning rather than against taste. It follows the design-rationale practice from the interaction-design course notes: explain the decisions, keep the design knowledge, and make the discipline visible. The principles applied are Nielsen's ten usability heuristics, the visual foundations from the first interaction-design lecture (information hierarchy, Gestalt proximity, small multiples, white space, grids, asymmetric typography, greyscale first, one or two colours, animation sparingly, keep it simple), and the second lecture's Norman principles, Dix et al. usability principles, Shneiderman's eight golden rules and universal design.

## Who the pages are for

The page now serves collections teams at Nigerian lenders and cooperatives, credit and risk reviewers, and SME finance teams. It answers: What is this? Which workflow fits my team? What can I try today? What remains outside the sandbox? What would a pilot involve and cost? The product overview gives each audience a starting point without making an SME read a lender-only pitch (Nielsen 2, information hierarchy).

Returning users can sign in from the header. Reviewers and potential partners can reach availability, boundaries, pricing, implementation notes and security information without entering the console. The pilot link opens an email draft to the agreed contact; the visitor chooses whether to send it.

## The conceptual model on the page

The brand descriptor is "Collections, credit and cash operations", in the same words in the page title, the lockup (header and footer) and its link label. Collections remains the operational foundation; Pay by Bank, Credit Desk and Cash Desk have their own tasks, permissions and review steps. The hero says "Collections, credit and cash. One clear view." and immediately states that Valo Pay never holds money. The product category is broader than collections, while the offered experience remains a synthetic sandbox.

The four sample journeys do not establish live bank connections, payment initiation, validated underwriting, accounting posting, tax filing or payouts. A grant to read accounts is not authority to perform those actions. The boundaries section, module descriptions and FAQ repeat the relevant limits near the choice they qualify; they do not imply that a future provider integration already exists.

Anonymous and signed-in sessions use the same console interface but separate workspaces. Signing in opens an account-linked workspace; it does not copy anonymous sandbox changes. The public copy makes this distinction explicit, and a sign-in does not enable live financial services. Anonymous sandboxes may be cleared after 30 days without changes; account-linked workspaces are not cleared by that inactivity rule.

## Information hierarchy and the visual foundations

- **The opening hierarchy.** The eyebrow names Nigerian lenders and small businesses; the H1 names collections, credit and cash; the description begins "Valo Pay never holds money." A separate availability strip identifies all four sample workspaces and links to live-use boundaries. The first screen establishes both product breadth and current availability.
- **One hierarchy of type.** One H1, one H2 per section and H3s for the jobs, process steps and audiences. Weight and size carry the hierarchy, so the page still reads in greyscale (design first in greyscale, then add colour).
- **Left-aligned, asymmetric layout.** The desktop hero pairs the main copy with a four-workspace illustrative showcase. Its tabs are real controls that change local sample content only, using arrow keys, Home and End as well as pointer selection. They do not read a bank account or create a sandbox. Each preview identifies its illustrative status and links to the corresponding real workspace.
- **Grid and spacing.** Four product cards describe the focused workspaces. The product tour has four screen choices, the process has three numbered steps and the audience section has three routes into the console. The boundaries, commercial scope and FAQ use paired desktop columns that stack as the viewport narrows. Section spacing and card padding maintain the relationship between a task, its explanation and its action.
- **Proximity.** What belongs together sits together: the icon, title and text of a card; the two buttons and their helper line; the artwork and its caption. White space separates sections, while fine process dividers and faint decorative contours support the reference's visual rhythm.
- **Small multiples.** Product cards use one pattern, process steps another and boundary statements a compact icon-and-copy pattern. The showcase changes the content to suit the actual task: payment stages, credit evidence or cash scenarios, rather than giving every module the same financial KPI card.
- **White space as an element.** Airy desktop sections become more compact on small screens. Preview content stays in document flow; grid details and the final call to action's orange arcs are decorative CSS, not remote image assets.
- **Colour.** The landing keeps scoped `--lp-*` tokens: warm paper, white cards, graphite ink and burnt orange, with dark equivalents. Status uses words as well as colour. Exact contrast outcomes must come from the current browser checks; the historical console ratios below do not establish contrast for new public components.
- **Motion.** The showcase never advances automatically and the tour never starts itself. Switching content follows an explicit user action. Focus and hover remain visible without an animated marketing sequence.
- **Named actions.** "Open the sandbox", "Discuss a pilot", workspace-specific Explore links and "Load interactive preview" identify different destinations. The hero's tour link reaches the working preview section; the header's How it works link reaches the three-step path.

## Product tour, commercial scope and questions

The hero showcase and the product tour have different purposes. The showcase is a local illustration. The tour embeds the actual Collections, Pay by Bank, Credit Desk or Cash Desk screen only after the visitor presses "Load interactive preview". Selecting another screen removes the iframe and requires another load choice. Closing a preview returns focus to that load button; it does not undo saved sample changes. The mounted deployment base path is respected, and a full-screen link offers more room on a phone. Preview load status is announced, and the selected workflow's limits remain alongside it.

How it works is a discovery sequence: start with a sample workflow, inspect evidence and permissions, then scope a pilot. It does not describe an automatic upgrade from sandbox access to live authority. The audience cards offer task-specific entry routes for lenders and cooperatives, credit and risk teams, and SME finance teams.

Pricing distinguishes the proposed Core Entry planning basis from a purchasable subscription. The displayed proposal is ₦150,000 monthly for fewer than 3,000 qualifying collections, ₦1,000,000 one-time implementation and 0.3% per eligible direct debit capped at ₦150. The ₦90 example on ₦30,000 is labelled as usage only; provider charges and applicable VAT are additional. Pay by Bank, Credit Desk and Cash Desk are separately scoped pilot capabilities, not an unlimited bundle included in the collections entry price. Complete terms require written agreement. No comparison with an assumed provider fee is used.

Native FAQ disclosures explain current availability, account separation, the limits of credit scores, the unconnected Paystack/Xero targets and live-pilot requirements. Footer links expose all four workspaces, Permissions & readiness, implementation documentation and security information. The pilot email draft asks for workflow, organisation, provider/software names, approximate volume and timing; it requests no customer records, bank details or credentials and sends nothing automatically.

## Nielsen's ten heuristics

| Heuristic | Where it shows |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Visibility of system status | The public availability strip, sample-data labels and workflow-specific limits distinguish current sandbox behaviour from live services. Showcase tabs expose their selected state; the tour announces loading and loaded states. Sign-in says whether authentication is available. In the console, waiting regions and in-flight buttons name their action; notices report completion or failure; the theme and print metadata retain their existing state descriptions. |
| 2. Match between system and the real world | Mandates, instalments, settlements, aggregators, NIBSS, WAT, exact naira amounts. British spelling, and the page says so (`lang="en-GB"`). Every time carries its zone, whatever the device is set to, and the customers' names are written with their Yoruba and Igbo marks, in the console, the PDF and the spreadsheet. No "leverage", "seamless" or "solution". A printout reads like a file note: no controls, no chrome, the lender and the sandbox notice at the top. |
| 3. User control and freedom | Reading the public landing page, changing its illustrative showcase or opening FAQ disclosures does not create a workspace. Opening a console link or explicitly loading the interactive preview can do so. The preview can be closed and loaded again; selecting another tour screen requires a fresh load choice. Sign-in offers the sandbox without an account and explains that anonymous work is not adopted. The existing skip links, notice dismissal and device-theme controls remain. |
| 4. Consistency and standards | One brand lockup, shared Button components and consistent workspace names across public pages and the console. | 5. Error prevention | No form is shown where sign-in cannot work. The sandbox is offered before an account is asked for. | 6. Recognition rather than recall | Workspace cards, audience links and tour choices name the same four products as the console. The tour suggests one concrete exercise before it loads. ## Norman's principles

- **Visibility and discoverability.** The ways in are visible in the header, the hero and the end of the page. No hamburger menu holds the only way in; on a phone the header keeps Sign in, and the hero's Open the sandbox is in the first screen.
- **Affordance and signifiers.** Buttons and links have distinct tasks. Hero tabs change an explicitly illustrative preview; the separate "Load interactive preview" button opens a working sample console. Selection and loading are not presented as financial actions.
- **Mapping.** Four showcase tabs, product cards, tour choices and console routes use the same workspace names. The three numbered steps describe sample exploration, evidence review and pilot scoping. The tour and How it works links lead to different, correctly named sections.
- **Feedback.** Hover and focus states on every control; the page title changes per page; when the sandbox opens the banner and the lender selector confirm where you are.
- **Constraints.** Reading and illustrative selection do not mutate records. Once explicitly loaded, the real tour can change sample records, and closing it does not reverse those changes. None of the new sample financial workflows performs a live financial operation.
- **Knowledge in the world.** The four workspaces, current availability, commercial assumptions and boundaries are visible without completing a form or requesting a deck.
- **Simplify the task structure.** Opening the sandbox is one click and needs no account; signing in is one form with no wizard around it.

## Dix et al.: learnability, flexibility, robustness

- **Learnability.** Predictability: each link's label is its destination. Synthesisability: the title and address change on every page, and the console's banner confirms the state. Familiarity: the words are the lender's own. Generalisability: the sign-in shell reuses the console's card, button and status chip, so what is learnt on one page applies to the next. Consistency: one lockup, one action colour, one voice.
- **Flexibility.** Visitors can inspect local examples, load a working preview, open a specific workspace or discuss a pilot. Anonymous and signed-in access are available without being described as interchangeable record stores. A full-screen tour route is available when an embedded console is inconvenient.
- **Robustness.** Explicit sample labels, selected states and load status explain what is on screen. The public landing does not request workspace data until a console entry or preview load is chosen. Authentication may contact Clerk; it is not a promise of zero network traffic. Older performance measurements below are historical, not proof that this refresh has no layout shift or a particular load time.

## Shneiderman's eight golden rules

Consistency uses the same workspace names across the public pages and console. Shortcuts include anchors, direct workspace links and keyboard showcase selection. Focus, selected state and preview load status provide feedback. The three-step discovery path and repeated entry choices provide closure. Authentication fallbacks and console error states retain a way forward. The user controls showcase selection, preview loading and email sending. Closing a preview is reversible as a viewing action but does not undo records already saved in the sample workspace; inactivity expiry is not an undo mechanism. Visible task exercises and account-separation copy reduce the need to remember hidden conditions.

## Universal design

- **Equitable.** The sandbox needs no account and costs nothing to look at.
- **Perceptible.** In the console, text is ink on the warm page background (about 14:1) or the muted grey (about 5.1:1); the brand orange is never text. The dark theme meets the same bar: 17:1 and 7.8:1 on its background, with the figures for the status colours under Dark mode below. On paper the light theme's ink is used whatever the screen showed, and status is carried by words and borders because backgrounds are not printed by default. Status is carried by words as well as colour, and a status word passes 4.5:1 on its own tint. Icons are hidden from assistive technology and always paired with text. Every control has a name that outlives its placeholder, and a frame that scrolls has a name while it scrolls. Landmarks: header, a labelled section nav, main, aside, footer; headings in order; a `figure` with its caption; the steps are an ordered list whose headings read "Step 1" to a screen reader.
- **Simple and intuitive.** Plain words, short sentences, one idea per line.
- **Tolerance for error.** Public reading, showcase selection and FAQ controls are reversible. Entering the real preview is deliberate; the console's own review and confirmation controls apply to sample mutations inside it.
- **Low physical effort.** Everything works by keyboard in reading order; buttons are at least 36 px high, and close buttons and link-styled buttons at least 24 px; no drag, hover-only or timed interaction; nothing moves for someone who has asked for less motion.
- **Size and space.** Public grids stack as their content needs more room; tour choices remain a compact two-column group on phones. The embedded console has a full-screen alternative. Preview labels and financial figures remain in document flow rather than being overlaid on a fixed bitmap. Existing console responsive rules retain the drawer, lender context, wrapped controls and internally scrolling tables described below.

## The sign-in page

Clerk's form is themed rather than rebuilt. It already handles every strategy the account is configured with, its error messages are plain language, and its labels stay visible. Rebuilding it would add a second place where account errors are worded and risk a form that looks like sign-in but is not one. The theming (`appearance` in `sign-in.tsx`) passes the console's colour tokens (the action orange, the ink, the card and the muted grey, so both themes are covered), radius and typeface, so the form belongs to the page.

The page pairs a navy welcome panel with a focused authentication card, using warm surfaces and orange accents in both themes. The heading "Sign in" and its introduction name the workspace and the records saved in the account. The supporting panel covers four products in three groups: Collections and Pay by Bank, Credit Desk and Cash Desk. It explains why signing in is useful without promising to copy anonymous changes. A native retention disclosure distinguishes anonymous inactivity cleanup from account-linked retention. The footer beside the form says that live payments and bank connections are switched off, even when you sign in, and the context repeats the synthetic-data and non-custody boundaries. On phones, the form follows the welcome before the longer supporting context. Sign-up, "Create an account", shares the shell with account-creation copy.

The authentication styles are scoped to the sign-in/sign-up frame. Clerk retains its Google/email flows, account links, validation and recovery screens; its form inputs and primary/social buttons are at least 46 px high, with visible focus styles. Input text is 16 px to avoid automatic form zoom on phones. No credential handling or authentication configuration is changed.

Where authentication is unconfigured, the page says "Sign-in is unavailable here" and offers the sample workspace plus a home link. It does not display a form that cannot work or expose configuration instructions to a visitor. Where configured, Clerk keeps its account, recovery and sign-up flows and uses `/overview` as the fallback destination after authentication.

## Routing

The public routes are outside the console's workspace provider (`App.tsx`). Reading `/`, `/sign-in` or `/sign-up` does not request the Valo workspace or create a sandbox; configured authentication can still contact Clerk. Console links and an explicitly loaded iframe mount the real workspace provider. The four tour targets are `/overview`, `/pay-by-bank`, `/credit-desk` and `/cash-desk`; `/connections` explains their permissions and readiness. Unknown public addresses remain outside the workspace provider. Fragment links preserve the established section destinations and are followed once, when they arrive: paging a table on that page afterwards keeps the view and focus with the table rather than returning to the section or the top of the page (Nielsen 3). The public mobile menu closes after selection and Escape returns focus to its toggle.

## Not found

There are two not-found states, and they are different pages because the visitor's situation is different. An address the console has no page for gets the public frame (the same header as the sign-in pages), the address it looked for shown exactly so a typo can be seen, a sentence that nothing changed, and two ways out: the overview and the start. It fetches nothing, so it renders at once and creates nothing. A customer address whose record the current lender does not have is a page that exists with a record that does not, so it stays inside the console with the sidebar and the lender selector, names the reference it looked for, says the customer may belong to another lender in the workspace, and links back to the customer list. Neither state shows a code or a stack trace, and each sets a page title that says what happened (Nielsen 1 and 9, Dix recoverability, Shneiderman's simple error handling).

## Errors

When a page throws, the console does not go blank and does not show a developer's message. The boundary inside the console layout catches it, so the sidebar and the lender selector stay as the way out, and the page area shows the same notice card as the not-found states with `role="alert"` so it is announced; the page title says "Page error" while the notice is up. It says what happened (the page stopped working), what it means (an error on a page changes no record, and Request history shows whether a change just saved reached Valo Pay), what to report if it happens again (the time and the address, shown exactly), and two ways on: Try again, which re-renders the page, and Open Overview, a plain link so it starts afresh. The old fallback said "This part of the app hit an error" in grey outside the design system and printed nothing a lender's staff could act on. The error's own message can carry an API response or another internal, so it is folded away under "Technical details" in development and not rendered in production. Boundaries outside the console (the router and the root) show the notice in the public frame with only Try again and Back to home, since a visitor there has saved no change and may have no workspace, and navigating to another address clears a caught error, so a broken page never traps the visitor (Nielsen 3 and 9, Dix recoverability, Shneiderman's simple error handling and easy reversal). The not-found states and the error notice share one card, `components/notice.tsx`, so every such moment reads the same way.

A refresh that fails is not a page that failed. Overview, Reports and Settings refresh every minute and, like the connected pages, after an action; when such a refresh fails, the figures already on the page stay, and a small notice above them says they could not be refreshed, when they were last updated (in West Africa Time, like every instant) and offers Try again (`components/load-problem.tsx`). Before this, one failed refresh replaced good figures, and on Settings the form of an open draft, with an error card. The full card is for a first load that failed, when there is nothing to show (Nielsen 1 and 9; Dix: robustness).

When the workspace itself cannot be loaded, and none was loaded before, nothing in the console can work, so the notice replaces the pages rather than sitting inside them, in the same frame and card. The words follow the situation instead of one catch-all sentence: when the service asks the visitor to wait (a new sandbox refused by its network's or the server's limit, or the request limit), its own plain-language message is passed on; when there is no answer, the page says the console could not reach the service and to check the connection; when the service hit an error, it says so and gives the time for a report, and keeps the body's wording to itself because a 5xx can carry internals. It makes no claim about what changed, since a save sent just before may have been recorded: it says to check Operations, once the workspace opens, before sending a change again. Try again shows its own progress ("Trying again…", disabled) so a click is seen to do something, and the frame keeps the start and, where sign-in exists, sign-in as the other ways on. The old state was one centred sentence with a retry button and no way out. Before the workspace arrives, the page area says "Loading your workspace…" as a status, under a heading that names the page for assistive technology, rather than showing pages with nothing in them (Nielsen 1: visibility of system status; 9; Dix: observability and recoverability; Shneiderman: informative feedback).

A workspace on the screen is refreshed every thirty seconds, and that refresh can fail too: the service is unreachable for a few seconds, or answers the request limit. That is not a workspace that cannot be loaded. Every page, form and dialog stays where it is, with its draft and any "Request not confirmed" check, and a notice above the page says "We could not refresh your workspace.", why in the service's words (the reference of a 5xx included), when the workspace on the screen was loaded, and that open pages and forms are kept; it links to Request history (the Operations page, `/operations`), where a save whose answer was lost can be checked before it is sent again, and offers Try again. When the service asks for a wait with `Retry-After` (a 429, or a 503 while a lender is busy), the next automatic refresh waits that long, and the notice says when; returning to the tab does not refresh sooner. A modal dialog keeps the page behind it inert, so its notice's Try again waits until the dialog is closed; the next automatic refresh clears the notice by itself. Before this, one failed refresh (the service down for about three seconds when the refresh ran, or a single 429) replaced the whole console with the unavailable notice, unmounted every open dialog, including the retry of a save that had in fact been recorded, and told the person that nothing had changed (`WorkspaceRefreshProblem` in `components/workspace-unavailable.tsx`; Nielsen 1, 3 and 9; Dix: robustness).

A read and a change fail differently, so they say different things. On the pilot pages (Pilot journey, Import batches, Close review, Data retention, Data sources, Request history, Team and access, My work, Saved exports and a case), a read that gets no answer, or only a proxy's error page, is headed "We could not load {thing}", as every other failed load is, and says "Valo Pay could not be reached. Check your connection and try again.": nothing was asked of the service but to read, so nothing waits anywhere. A change that gets no answer says where to check it: Request history, for the changes it records, and the page itself for team, access and new lender changes, which Request history does not record. Since the page is then the only place to check such a change, Team and access, its staff pilot set-up checks and the pilot journey's lender set-up ask before they are left or reloaded while one is being sent or its outcome is unconfirmed, as Settings does. The notices of an unconfirmed change in the record, review and mandate dialogs and on Settings now say, as the pilot pages' notices already did, that a request Valo Pay received stays in Request history after the form is closed or the page reloaded, with Open Request history beside them; the demo role switch, which Request history does not record, keeps its notice without the link. Before, every one of them said "Your original request is available in Operations if it reached the server", to a person who had made no request or whose request Operations would never list, and a correction preview's own check ("Change a value before previewing the correction.") was replaced by that sentence (`PilotError` in `components/pilot-ui.tsx`; Nielsen 2 and 9).

## Loading and waiting

Waiting is a state like any other, so it is said, not left blank. Every region that waits for the API shows one status line, `Loading <what>…`, that names what is coming ("Loading the overview…", "Loading due items…"), left-aligned in the space the content will take, with `role="status"` so it is announced once. The only motion on the page is the small spinner beside it, and it stops under reduced motion. A table that is waiting says so in its own row, so the header and the columns are already in place. Before this the console had five wordings across twenty-two places ("Loading...", "Generating reports..." for a fetch, "Loading audit logs..."), three paddings, centred text, an optional pulse and no status role.

No skeletons. A line of words is simpler, the same everywhere, honest about how long it may take, and it never pretends to know the shape of what is coming; a pulse of grey boxes is animation that draws the eye for no information (animation sparingly; keep it simple).

Every state of a page has an h1, so a screen-reader user who moves by heading, and the whole-page accessibility check, find where the page starts. A page that shows nothing but its waiting line ("Loading your workspace…", "Loading the page…", "Loading the overview…", the connected pages and a customer's history) gives that line as the heading, which also keeps the page's own heading meaning that the page has arrived; a first load that failed shows the page's own heading above the problem card: the overview's, and the connected pages' with their module tabs, so the other modules stay one step away.

A button whose action is in flight is disabled, `aria-busy`, and its label says what is happening beside a spinner: Saving…, Generating…, Verifying…, Running the engine…, Running daily close…, Trying again…. This is one property of the button (`busy` with `busyLabel`) rather than a ternary rewritten in each page, so the feedback is the same everywhere and the click cannot be repeated while it runs. Where several buttons share one action, such as the dispute pack's PDF, CSV and JSON, only the one that was pressed says it is generating and the others wait, disabled (Nielsen 1: visibility of system status; Dix: responsiveness and observability; Shneiderman: informative feedback; universal design: perceptible information, reduced motion).


## Empty states

An empty list is a message, so it says three things in order: what would be here, why it is empty here and now, and what to do next when there is something to do. "No proposals waiting for review" is followed by what a proposal is and that running the engine looks for new ones; "No mandates yet" says where mandates come from and offers the sandbox's own way to create one; "No daily close yet" points at the button above and the scheduled close. The words are the lender's (instalment, mandate, settlement batch, notice) and every state that has a rule behind it names the rule (REC-09, BIL-01, MEA-05), so the empty state also teaches how the console fills. Before this there were about twenty-six of them with mixed wording ("Log empty", "No authorizations found for this workspace", "No close snapshots available."), centred, some with a large faded icon, and none said what to do.

"Nothing yet" and "nothing matches" are different situations. A search or a filter with no results is the outcome of what the visitor just did, so it says so with the term it looked for ("No customers match “zzzz”", "No resolved exceptions yet"), suggests how to widen it, and carries `role="status"` so it is announced; a list that is genuinely empty is not announced, because nothing happened. The customers and audit pages used to show "No customers found" and "Log empty" for both.

One component, `components/empty-state.tsx`, with a table-row form, left-aligned in the space the content will take, without an illustration: the words are the information, and a faded icon adds nothing a reader can act on (Nielsen 1, 2, 6 and 9; Norman: knowledge in the world; Dix: observability; Shneiderman: closure; keep it simple).


## Forms and validation

A form that refuses a value says which one, where it is, and what would be accepted. Every form in the console (the record dialog that most pages use, the mandate form, the execution settings) now checks its fields before asking the server: a missing value is named at the field in the field's own words ("Enter the Full Name.", "Choose the Customer.", "Enter the close time as HH:MM in West Africa Time, for example 07:00."), the control is marked invalid at its edge and in the accessibility tree, the message is linked to it so a screen reader reads both together, focus moves to the first field that needs attention, and one alert at the top says how many fields need attention so the situation is announced once. A field being corrected drops its message at once. Nothing is sent until the form is complete, so a slip never costs a request or a round trip (Nielsen 5: error prevention; 9: plain words, the problem stated precisely, a constructive way out; Dix: recoverability; Shneiderman: simple error handling; universal design: perceptible, never colour alone).

An edit that empties an optional field clears it. An emptied field used to be left out of the request and the old value stayed, while the dialog closed as if it had been saved; it now sends the field as null, which the API reads as "remove this field" (Nielsen 1 and 5). An edit sends only the fields the dialog shows: the API merges them over the stored data, so what the service recorded beside them stays as it is. Before, every edit sent the record's stored data back whole, so a settlement batch of about 5,000 lines or more, whose line lists then passed the API's cap on the values in a request, could no longer be edited. An exception's severity is not optional: every exception type has one, which the service gives a new exception, so the edit dialog requires it and never clears it (the console creates no exception itself). An exception stored without one, as an earlier edit could leave it, shows "No severity set" on Exceptions rather than LOW, since the queue ranks it below low and the High filter leaves it out (Nielsen 1 and 2).

What only the server can refuse is treated the same way. Its validation details name fields by their path in the request, and each one is placed under the field it names; a rule or a role refusal that names no field becomes the alert's title in the server's own plain words. The execution settings do the same with the server's rule messages, which begin with the key they concern, so "closeTime must be…" sits under the close time rather than in a toast that disappears. Before this, the record dialog listed raw field paths below the form, the mandate form printed the HTTP status line, and the settings page announced a rejection in a toast and then hid it.

Native browser validation is switched off on these forms because its bubbles differ by browser, cannot be styled to match, and are not announced consistently; the `required` attribute stays for its meaning. Required fields keep the conventional asterisk, and a button that waits for a value says why (the kill switch's reason is required and recorded in the audit log).

A button submits its form only when it says so. The shared Button is a plain button unless it is marked `type="submit"`, as only a form's own submit button is. Before, every Button inside a form was a submit button: Previous and Next in the allocation picker and in the mandate form's customer picker sent the allocation or created the mandate when the form was complete, and named the missing fields when it was not, and Check original request, in the pilot pages' forms, submitted its form as well as checking the original request. A picker's search looks as the person types, so Enter there does nothing more; before, it submitted the form around it. Every console test checks the forms it renders and fails when a control inside one can submit it without being its submit button (`tests/form-submitters.ts`) (Nielsen 5; Norman: constraints).

A typed draft is not lost by leaving: a link, the lender selector, closing the tab and the browser's Back and Forward buttons all ask first, and Cancel keeps the page and the draft. Back and Forward are heard through a listener on the window that must run before the router's own, which re-renders the page; listeners on the window run in the order they were added, and Chromium keeps that order even for a listener that captures, so the guard is installed when the app loads, before the router subscribes, rather than after the first render. Before this, in Chromium, Back unmounted the draft first and the guard, asked afterwards, found nothing to protect; jsdom, which runs a capturing listener first, hid the fault. A browser test (`e2e/console-state.spec.ts`) now checks Back and Forward with a draft in Chromium (Nielsen 3 and 5; Shneiderman: permit easy reversal of actions).


## Notifications

A notice after an action is either a confirmation or a problem, and the two behave differently on purpose. A done notice says what happened in plain words and where the result is ("Dispute pack generated. It opened in a new tab. SHA-256 …, generated 18 Sept 2026, 09:08"), is announced politely so it does not interrupt, and goes away on its own after six seconds, pausing while pointed at or focused. A problem notice says what did not happen and passes on the server's own words ("The dispute pack was not generated. Packs are limited to five a day for this customer. Nothing has been changed."), is announced at once, and stays until dismissed, because an error that disappears before it is read is no error message at all. Before this every notice, errors included, vanished after five seconds, the dismiss control was invisible until hovered, and one notice replaced another. The dismiss control is now always visible and named, three notices can stand at once, and nothing animates under reduced motion.

Two decisions follow from the same rule that a notice is for what the page cannot show. The audit page shows the result of a verification in its own status box, so it no longer raises a notice for it, only for a request that failed. The dispute pack opens in a new tab, which the page cannot show, so its notice says so and carries an Open action for a browser that kept the tab closed. And no notice ever carries an HTTP status line: `saidBy` passes on the server's sentence or a plain fallback (Nielsen 1, 3, 8 and 9; Shneiderman: informative feedback, easy reversal; universal design: perceptible information, low physical effort).

Resolving an exception takes it out of the open queue, so Exceptions shows the service's answer in a box of its own above the queue: which exception was resolved, and what the service did, such as "Exception resolution recorded. The next reconciliation adds this payment evidence to payment PSK-SET-1. No second payment is created." Before, the answer was dropped and the page showed nothing. The resolve dialog says what the chosen outcome does wherever the service acts on it: for payment evidence held as a suspected duplicate, that the next reconciliation joins it to its payment (only while it is held for its connection alone), sets it aside for good, or makes it a payment of its own, a reversal being set aside instead; for a payment held as a suspected duplicate, that Separate payments releases it at once; for a reversal waiting for a payment no connection has seen, that Provider status accepted keeps it waiting to reverse that payment when it arrives and Valo Pay status kept sets it aside for good. A mandate's or attempt's mismatch offers those two codes under the same names, which say nothing of a reversal, so wherever Exceptions names them for a waiting reversal (the dialog's choices, its outcome box and a resolved exception's row) each also says what it does: "Provider status accepted; reversal waits for its payment" and "Valo Pay status kept; reversal set aside permanently". No code keeps that exception open while Finance checks with the provider, so before one is chosen the dialog says to leave it open meanwhile, and what a payment that arrives meanwhile does: the reversal applies to it only when it comes through the reversal's own connection and agrees with it, and is otherwise held with an exception of its own, while this exception closes either way (where it once promised that the reversal applied to any such payment). Elsewhere it says, as before, that resolving records the outcome and reason and moves no money; before, it said so for these too (Nielsen 1 and 2). A settlement batch's exception can also carry the provider's report of a collection counted in two batches (`countedTwice`), which Finance's resolution settles, whatever its outcome, so the report is not raised again; the dialog then says so beside the outcome, before and after a code is chosen, and asks for both payouts to be checked first, where before it said only that resolving records the outcome.


## Keyboard

Everything in the console works without a mouse, and the keyboard paths are the same on every page. The first tab stop on every page, public or console, is a skip link to the page's content, so a keyboard user is not made to pass the banner, the lender selector and eleven links each time. After in-app navigation focus moves to the page's main region, as it would on a page load, so keyboard and screen-reader users start at the top of what changed rather than on a link that may no longer exist; the current page's link carries `aria-current`, so "current page" is announced and not only coloured. Every sidebar link shows a focus ring.

When a dialog closes, focus goes back to the control that opened it. A confirmed step often removes its own button (simulating the authorisation of a Pay by Bank checkout removes Simulate authorisation; setting up the Cash Desk removes its set-up button); then focus goes to the message that says what happened, and only without one to the page's main region, never to the page body, where a keyboard user would have to start again from the top (`useDialogFocusReturn` in `lib/focus.ts`). Buttons outside a dialog that remove themselves do the same: Edit on Settings moves focus to the form's first field, and Cancel or a save puts it back on Edit; a Pay by Bank step taken without a review, such as Simulate return from bank, moves it to the message that says what the step did, or why it was refused, once focus has fallen to the page (`useFocusWhenLost`); on Team and access, Approve invitation, Approve change and Reject change, which remove their item, and a revoked invitation, saved lender access or a suspension or role change, whose form the new version replaces, move it to the message that says what happened; on Settings, Approve turning it off and Keep the stop on, whose box goes when they succeed, move it to what the request did, or to the notice of an answer that was lost; End presentation moves it to the page's content. Before, each of these left focus on the page body. Clear page search, in the sidebar and in the drawer, goes once the search is cleared and puts focus back in Find a page, where before it fell to the page body or, in the drawer, to the drawer itself; the count of pages found is in a live region that is on the page before the first search, so a screen reader announces the first count as well as later ones. Resolving an exception, whose Resolve button goes with it, moves focus to the service's answer shown above the queue, where before it fell to the page body or the main region. A message that says what happened also takes focus from the page's main region, where a dialog leaves it when the message has not arrived yet: Revoke access on Team and access, whose change replaces the member's form with its new version, leaves focus there until the answer arrives and then on the member's message that says what the revocation did, where before it stayed on the main region; a revocation or other access change, a lender access change included, that the service refuses, or whose answer is lost, moves it in the same way to the member's notice that says so, where before it stayed on the main region (for a lender access change, on the page body). A member's card watches only its latest request, and only while the focus stays in the card (`useFocusWhenLost` with the card as its scope): an answer that arrives after the person has moved to another card or to the invitation form leaves the focus where they are, where before an earlier change's refusal on one card could take the focus from a revocation on it or from an invitation. Discard original request, whose notice goes with it, moves focus to the control that sent the discarded request, which the page names where it can (Approve turning it off, Keep the stop on and the main emergency-stop button on Settings; on Data retention the run's Execute or Resume button, or the control that sent a policy, hold, preview or approval), and otherwise to the submit button of the form the notice is in, or to the page's own nearest control before the notice or, with none, after it (`components/discard-original-request.tsx`); before, it left focus on the page body. That search stays within the page's own content: on Data retention it once found the Sandbox guide at the top of the page, the only control above the run's notice. Paging by keyboard keeps focus on the pager control pressed, in every paged list: Customers, the Audit log, the Exceptions, Mandates and Collections queues, Reconciliation's six tables, the close history on Reports, a customer's four sections, the allocation picker, the mandate form's customer picker, and the Previous and Next buttons of Request history, Import batches, Saved exports, My work and Data retention's kept items. Each keeps its rows, its pager and the control pressed until the next page arrives, and never shows another lender's, record's, search's or filter's rows while one loads (`keepRowsWhilePaging` in `lib/use-record-pagination.ts`), nor the previous page's rows once the next page's request has failed: fetching that page again (Try again, the automatic refresh of Saved exports and Request history, a return to the tab) shows it loading, where before it showed the previous page's rows under the new page's range. When the next page fails, the problem notice that takes the pager's place takes its focus too, its Try again or, where it has none (Reconciliation's tables, whose Refresh queue sits above them), the notice itself, and takes it again if a later fetch of the page fails again before the person moves on; before, focus fell to the page body, or in a picker to the top of its dialog (`usePageProblemFocus` in `components/record-pagination.tsx`). Only the notice of the list whose pager was pressed takes it: each list's notice names the pager it replaces, and another list's notice, a change's problem or a first load's never does, so a failed page of the allocation picker keeps the focus inside its dialog, where it once went to the notice of a table behind it. Try again presses the pager again as it fetches, since the list shows its page loading meanwhile, so the control pressed takes the focus back when the page arrives, and the notice's Try again when it fails again; before, Try again took its own notice away and focus fell to the page body. While the page loads the controls stay focusable and wait, marked unavailable and ignoring presses; a picker shows its loading line beside its pager rather than in its place; a page button that the first or last page disables passes focus to the other one; and My work, once its page arrives, moves focus to its list's heading, as before (`components/record-pagination.tsx`, whose `PageButtons` give the pilot pages' Previous and Next the same rules). Before, only Reconciliation's tables kept their rows: paging any other list replaced its rows or its pager with a loading line, so focus fell to the page body, and paging a picker left it at the top of its dialog.

Two accelerators exist for the people who use the console all day, and neither is required: "/" puts the caret in the search box on the pages that have one, with a small "/" hint at the edge of the box so it can be found rather than guessed, and Escape clears the box. The exception filter tabs follow the WAI-ARIA tabs pattern: one tab stop for the group, the arrow keys and Home and End move the selection and the focus together, and the list follows. Dialogs, menus and notices already trap and return focus and close on Escape through their primitives, and F8 jumps to the notices. The shortcuts are listed in a Keyboard section on the settings page, because help the reader has to guess at is no help (Nielsen 4, 6, 7 and 10; Shneiderman: shortcuts for frequent users, consistency; universal design: operable by keyboard, low physical effort).

## Responsive layout

The console is used at a desk, but a collections lead checks an exception from a phone and a reviewer opens a shared link on a tablet, and the same page has to work in each place without a second design to learn. One layout adapts: below 768 px the sidebar folds into a drawer behind an Open menu button, and above it the sidebar returns. Nothing is added or taken away in between; things move.

**The phone bar.** It holds the three things a page cannot do without: the brand mark (the way back to the start, the same lockup as the sidebar, with the name shown once there is room for it), the lender selector, and the Open menu button. The lender stays in the bar rather than in the drawer because every figure on every page belongs to one lender, and a value that changes the meaning of the page must stay visible (Norman: visibility; Nielsen 1). The button says "Open menu" in words beside its icon ("Menu" below 360 px, so the lender's name keeps its room, with "Open menu" still its name), because an icon alone is a guess (Keep it simple: no icon-only buttons). The bar stays at the top of the screen, so on a long page the menu and the lender are never a scroll away.

**A drawer, not a select.** The console used to offer its pages on a phone as a `<select>`. A select is compact, but it hides the list until it is opened, shows no icons, cannot mark the current page the way the sidebar does, and on the customer timeline had no page to show at all. The drawer lists the same pages, in the same named groups and order, with the same icons and the same current-page mark as the sidebar, so what is learnt at a desk holds on a phone (Nielsen 4 and 6; Dix: generalisability; Shneiderman: consistency). It opens with focus on the current page's link, so the reader starts where they are, however far down the list that is. Its rows are 44 px tall because they are pressed with a thumb (universal design: size and space). It is a dialog: it takes focus when it opens; Escape, its close button (Close menu) or a tap outside closes it and returns focus to the Open menu button; choosing a page closes it and puts focus on the page content, which is exactly what the sidebar does, so there is one keyboard path (Keyboard, above). If the window grows past the breakpoint while it is open it closes, since the button that would close it is no longer shown.

**Tables scroll within their frame, never the page.** A table wider than a phone is allowed to be wide; a collections table with nine columns should not be rebuilt as cards that hide six of them. Each table sits in its own horizontally scrolling frame, so the heading, the filters and the rest of the page stay put and only the table moves; the browser makes the frame reachable from the keyboard when it overflows. Two lists on the reconciliation page scrolled vertically only and now scroll both ways.

**Rows wrap, grids collapse, dialogs fit.** Rows of buttons (the reports header, an experiment's actions) wrap onto a second line rather than run off the page; the settings' two-column groups are one column below 640 px; the persona and emergency controls stack into a column with each control full width, so the kill switch's button is on the screen and not to the right of the reason box where a phone could not show it; the exception filter tabs wrap rather than being clipped by their card. A tablet is the narrow case in disguise: from 768 px the sidebar takes 256 px of the width, so the settings' pairs of fields split again only from 1280 px and the emergency controls stay stacked until 1024 px, because a reason box three characters wide is no box at all. Dialogs fit within 90% of the viewport height and scroll inside.

**One banner, the same warning at every width.** The banner says "Sample data only. Valo Pay never holds money. Live payments and bank connections are switched off." on a phone as at a desk, wrapping rather than dropping a statement; the environment badge, which said the sandbox again, is gone. Page padding is 16 px on a phone, 24 px on a tablet and 32 px at a desk, from the same spacing scale as everything else.

**A presentation guide that leaves the page in view.** The guide's talking point keeps at least 16rem of its row, so where the row is too narrow for it and its two buttons (a phone, or a tablet beside the sidebar) the buttons move under it rather than squeezing the title to a word a line, and on a phone its padding and spacing are tighter. At 390 by 664 px the guide used to fill about 60% of the screen with the title in six lines, so the customer history the presenter was showing began below the fold; now the title takes a line or two and the guide at most half the screen.

What we did not do: no separate phone pages, no hidden columns, no menu icon without a word beside it, no gesture without a button that does the same. At 200% zoom on a laptop the console becomes the phone layout, which is the point of having one layout (universal design: flexibility in use).

## Dark mode

The console follows the device. Someone whose phone or laptop is set to dark gets a dark console without being asked, because the device already holds the answer and a question would be one more thing between them and the page (Nielsen 7; universal design: flexibility in use). Someone who wants the console to differ from the device chooses Light or Dark on the settings page, and the choice is kept in that browser: it belongs to the person and the screen, not to the workspace, so it is not a setting the API knows about, it works without an account and before any request, and two people sharing a workspace do not fight over it. "Follow the device" gives the choice back (Nielsen 3). The settings page says which theme is showing and why, "Following the device: dark now", so the state is never a guess (Nielsen 1).

**One set of tokens, two values each.** Every colour in the console is a token (`--background`, `--card`, `--muted-foreground` and so on), and the dark theme is the same tokens with dark values, applied by one class on the root element. There is no second stylesheet and no per-page override, so a component looks right in both themes because it was written against the tokens once (Nielsen 4). Status colours were the exception: the sandbox banner, the severity and confidence pills, the alert tints and the warning icons used fixed ambers, blues, reds and greens, which would have stayed as light strips and pale chips on a dark page. They are tokens now: for warning and for info a surface, its text, its border and a strong colour for icons and words on the page, each with a light value that is the colour it always had and a dark value chosen for contrast. Success became a colour that passes as text: the old green was 2.2:1 on white, and "proven", "SIGNED" and the allocated amount were written in it; it is now 7.2:1 in light and 11:1 in dark.

**Contrast in both themes.** On the dark background the main text is 17:1 and the muted grey 7.8:1; the strong warning, info and success colours are 10.8:1, 7.3:1 and 10.5:1; the text on the warning and info surfaces is 13.5:1 and 12.1:1 in dark and 8.3:1 and 8.5:1 in light; the destructive red is 6.0:1 in light and, in dark, 5.6:1 on the card and 4.9:1 on its own tint over it, where the destructive button carries dark text for the same reason the success button does. The muted grey is 5.1:1 on the light page background and 5.4:1 on a card. The brand orange moves from 50% to 55% lightness in the dark theme so the mark keeps its weight on a dark card, and it is still never used for text (Colour, above).

**No flash, and the door matches the room.** A few lines in the page shell apply the choice before the first paint, with the same rule and key as the module the app uses, so a dark device never sees a white page first; a test pins the two together. Native controls follow through `color-scheme`, so a select or a scrollbar is not a light box on a dark page. The sign-in form, which is Clerk's, receives its dark base theme and the console's dark tokens when the console is dark.

What we did not do: no schedule (dark after sunset), because the device does that better; no third high-contrast theme, since both themes meet 4.5:1 for text; no per-page or per-lender colour, because the lender is chosen by name, not by hue.

## Print

A page gets printed when it leaves the console: a customer's timeline for a file, the day's exceptions for a meeting, an audit extract for a question from an auditor. Paper cannot be scrolled, clicked or switched, so the print rules keep what a reader needs later and drop what an operator needs now (Nielsen 2: the printout reads like a file note, not a screenshot; Nielsen 8: nothing on the page that cannot be used on paper).

**What goes.** The sidebar, the phone bar, the banner, every button, field, tab and menu, and every notice and dialog. Links keep their words and lose their underline, because a name in a table is information and a link on paper is not. The settings page's appearance and keyboard help stay on the screen, where they are of use.

**What opens.** Every table scrolls within its frame on screen; on paper a frame that scrolls is a table cut in half, so the frames open to full width, long identifiers wrap, and the header row repeats at the top of each page. Height limits on rendered lists are removed. Paginated tables and timelines print only the current page, with a range and total and an explicit notice when other pages are omitted; printing does not fetch or render the other pages. Rows, list items and figures are not split across pages, and a heading is not left at the foot of a page without what it names. On paper the overview's four metrics take two columns, however many the screen shows, since four across the page would leave an amount too little room; its two queues keep their two columns, since the paper is wide enough for them and the reader expects the screen's arrangement.

**What paper says that the screen said elsewhere.** The banner and the sidebar carried the sandbox notice and the lender; on paper a line at the top carries both ("Valo Pay · Sandbox" or "Valo Pay · Your workspace", the lender, "Sample data only. Valo Pay never holds money. This is not a live payment record or a statement of account."), and a line at the foot says when the page was printed, from which page and for which lender. The time is taken again as the print dialog opens, because a page can sit open for a day before someone prints it (Nielsen 1: a printout that says what it is and when). Where a list was filtered or searched, the filter is printed as words ("Showing: High severity (1)", "Search: “Ada”"), so the reader knows the page is a part and which part.

**Ink.** A printout is always the light theme: the dark values and the dark variant are scoped to screens, so a dark console prints dark ink on white rather than pale ink on a background the printer would leave out. Backgrounds are not forced, and the page does not need them: every status is carried by a word and every group by a border (greyscale first). Type is set at 11 pt on the console's own scale, with A4 margins of 16 by 14 mm.

**What we left out.** No address printed after each link (a sandbox address on paper helps no one); no "Print this page" button (the browser has one, and a second is one more control to explain); no separate print layout per page (one set of rules over the same markup, so a page that works on screen works on paper without a second design to keep in step).

## Accessibility audit

This section preserves the earlier console audit and its measurements. It is not an accessibility certification or a report of the 22 September public-page refresh. The new showcase, tour states, disclosures and authentication layouts require current browser and keyboard verification before release.

The pages were built against the principles above; the audit checks them against the standard, WCAG 2.2 at level AA, with axe-core over every public page, every console page and the states a page can be in (the record dialog, the settings form, a filtered list, the phone drawer), in both themes and at desk and phone widths, followed by checks axe does not make: heading levels, landmark names, a keyboard walk that records whether each stop shows its focus, the size of every target, and what still moves when the device asks for less motion. The findings, and what changed:

- **Content outside a landmark.** The sandbox banner and the phone bar sat above the landmarks. They are now the page's header, so a screen-reader user who moves by landmark meets the sandbox notice first, as a sighted user does (WCAG 1.3.1; Nielsen 1).
- **Controls without names.** The execution settings' labels were beside their fields but not attached to them; the persona and import selects and the kill-switch reason had no name at all; the two search boxes had a placeholder for a name, which goes as soon as something is typed. Every label now points at its control, every select and field has a name, the reason has a visible label, and the search boxes are named whatever they hold (WCAG 1.3.1, 3.3.2, 4.1.2).

## Performance

The numbers in this section are historical before-and-after measurements from the earlier console optimisation. They are retained to explain those implementation decisions, not as current bundle or load-time claims for the connected-product landing page.

Measured again on 23 September 2026 with `PORT=5173 BASE_PATH=/ pnpm --filter @workspace/valopay run build` (Vite 7.3.6): the entry chunk, which still carries the landing page and the shell, is 530 kB (529,962 bytes; 160 kB compressed), against the 466 kB (141 kB compressed) recorded below, and Vite warns that it is larger than its 500 kB chunk limit. Every console page and the sign-in pages are still chunks of their own.

Measured again on 27 September 2026 with the same command: the landing page and the shell now arrive as two files that load together, the entry (168.81 kB, 51.16 kB compressed) and a chunk of the modules it shares with pages loaded later, which Vite names after the first of them, `theme` (275.28 kB, 88.93 kB compressed), and which the page shell asks for beside the entry (`modulepreload`). Together they are 444.09 kB (140.09 kB compressed), within 3.2 kB of the entry's After figure of 23 September in the table below, so a build report's entry file alone understates what the first page loads.

Responsiveness is one of Dix's three parts of robustness, and a lender's staff reach the console on the connections they have. The audit measured before changing anything: the bundle's composition from its source map, and in Chromium, cold loads of the landing page and the console (requests, bytes, first paint, largest paint, layout shift, long tasks), the same on a slow connection with a slow processor (1.6 Mb/s down, 150 ms latency, the processor at a quarter), a walk through the pages counting the requests each made, and a timeline of the first visit to a page. Then the changes the numbers justified, and the measurements again.

**What was found.** One script of 712 kB (201 kB compressed) carried every page, and the landing page, which is for a visitor on an unknown connection, carried the whole console: on the slow connection it painted at 4.8 s and the overview at 5.6 s. The typefaces were asked for from inside the stylesheet, so the browser learned of them only after the stylesheet had arrived, and the page shell also asked for a typeface nothing uses. Every return to a page fetched its data again, so a walk between three pages made a request for the overview three times in twenty seconds. A tooltip provider sat at the root of the app for tooltips that no page shows. The scrolling frames from the accessibility audit created a new observer on every render.

**What changed.** The landing page and the shell ship in one chunk of 466 kB (141 kB compressed); every console page and the sign-in pages are their own chunks, fetched on first visit; the remaining pages are fetched while the browser is idle, the overview from the landing page and every page from the console, unless the browser asks to save data or reports a 2G connection. The typefaces are linked from the page shell, in parallel with the stylesheet, and the unused one is gone. Data fetched in the last thirty seconds is shown at once when a page is returned to, instead of a loading line and a repeated request; an action's invalidation still refetches what it changed, and a tab that comes back after longer refetches on focus. The tooltip provider is gone. Each scrolling frame observes once.

**A hold that was not the network.** With the pages split, the first visit to a page took 330 ms on a fast connection although its code arrived in 20 ms. The timeline showed why: React commits a Suspense fallback and then holds it for 300 ms before the content may replace it, to avoid a flicker. The pages therefore load through plain state instead of Suspense: the page area says "Loading the page…" until the code is here and renders the page the moment it is, and a chunk that cannot be fetched is thrown to the error boundary. The first visit to a page now renders 40 ms after the click.

**Before and after.** Landing page: 712 kB of script became 466 kB; first paint on the slow connection 4.8 s became 3.5 s; on a fast one 384 ms became 380 ms. Overview: largest paint on the slow connection 5.6 s became 4.7 s. A page returned to within thirty seconds: one request became none, and the page appears at once. A page visited for the first time: 330 ms became 40 ms after the click. The overview's own largest paint on a fast connection moved from 968 ms to about 1,000 ms, the one round trip its chunk now costs on a direct visit; the prefetch removes it for every page visited after the first. No layout shift above 0.02 on any page, and no long task on a fast connection.

## Internationalisation

Norman's conceptual model and Nielsen's second heuristic both ask that the system speak the user's language, and for a Nigerian lender that language has conventions a browser will not supply on its own: British English words, day-month-year dates on a 24-hour clock, the naira with its sign, West Africa Time, and names written with the marks their bearers use (Adéyẹmí, Ọbi, Ṣeyi). The audit read every place the console and the API turn a value into words, set the browser to other languages and zones (German in New York, American English in Los Angeles) to see what changed, and looked at what the typefaces and the exports can spell.

**What was found.** Dates and amounts already had one locale and one zone (the formatters fix Africa/Lagos, British English for dates and Nigerian English for money), so a viewer in New York saw the same "18 Sept 2026, 13:50" as a viewer in Lagos; but no timestamp said which zone it was in, so the New York viewer had no way to know the time was not theirs, and a value that was only a day (a due date) was shown with an invented time of 01:00. One table formatted a number with the browser's own locale (20.000 in a German browser). Counts were built as "N items", so the audit log reported "1 entries" and a close "1 observations received". A search compared letters exactly, so "obi" could not find "Ọbi", nor "adeyemi" "Adéyẹmí". The synthetic names carried no marks at all, which hid the next two findings: the dispute pack's PDF used the standard Helvetica, which encodes WinAnsi only, and printed "?" for every ẹ, ọ or ṣ in a name, on a document meant as evidence of who was written to; and the CSV exports, UTF-8 without a byte order mark, open in a spreadsheet program as mojibake for the same letters. The page declared its language as generic English.

**What changed.** Every timestamp names its zone ("18 Sept 2026, 13:50 WAT"), and a day is shown as a day. The formatters carry the policy in one place (`lib/formatters.ts`): British English for words and dates, Nigerian conventions for numbers and money, West Africa Time for every instant whatever the device is set to; the one stray `toLocaleString()` now goes through them. A count helper gives a noun its number ("1 entry", "0 items", "1,234 records"), in the console and in the API's close summaries, invoice statements, alerts and packs alike. A search folds case and marks on both sides, so a name is found however it was typed. The synthetic lenders' customers are written as their bearers write them (Túndé, Ọbi, Adéyẹmí, Ifẹ, Ṣeyi), so every screen, export and test meets those letters. The PDF exports embed their own typeface, a Latin subset of Liberation Sans that is metrically the same as Helvetica, so nothing moved on the page, with the dot-below letters, the tone marks and the naira sign; it travels as base64 in a source module, since the repository's snapshot tool carries source text only, and the documents declare their language too. CSV downloads start with the byte order mark, which the importer already skipped. The page declares `lang="en-GB"`, the spelling it uses, so a screen reader picks a British voice and hyphenation and quotation follow British rules; Nigerian English was considered, and no assistive technology carries a voice for it, so the tag would have fallen back to generic English.

**Counts, percentages and large amounts.** A later audit found figures that still bypassed the formatters: counts written as they came ("1234 pending", "1 sources", "1 records linked"), percentages built with `toFixed` and a `%` (the match accuracy review, the pilot evidence, the recovery experiment, basis points on Reports, Evidence and the Cash Desk forecast), and money divided by 100 as a float, which shows a kobo too many or too few above about ₦70 trillion (₦90,071,992,547,409.91, the largest amount the console accepts, showed as .90). Every count, total and percentage now goes through `formatNumber`, `formatCount` or `formatPercent`, in Nigerian conventions like the rest, and `formatKobo` works in whole kobo, naira and kobo apart, keeping the currency's own sign and symbol, so ordinary amounts read exactly as before. Step numbers in the guides, the four review tasks and a form's field count stay as written: they are small fixed counts of the interface, not figures. The 23 September audit found the last few: an import batch's source quality ("1 source rows", "1 newly imported rows") and its check results ("1 rows to fix", "1 valid rows") now go through the formatters too, and a customer batch, whose rows carry no amounts, shows no "₦0.00 source total". Saved exports are named by what they hold and their format ("Dispute pack (JSON)", the name the service saves, `exportName` in the shared schema) and their state in the words of the status filter ("Ready to download", "Failed"), rather than by their machine name and status ("dispute-pack · JSON · ready"); the recent exports listed beside an export button read the same words from the same list (`exportStatusLabel` in `components/export-job-control.tsx`). Before the first fortnightly review, Go-live evidence says that a review is recorded in the signed-in person's name, as its dialog records it, instead of asking to name the reviewer. When no answer arrives, a recovery notice says "No answer came back from Valo Pay." instead of the browser's own "Failed to fetch" (`errorWords` in `lib/notify.tsx`), and the invitation page names itself in the browser's title.

**Money in another currency.** A payment in another currency is shown in that currency, and how many decimals its minor unit has comes from ISO 4217, through one table the API's messages and the console share (`currencyMinorUnit` in `lib/valopay-schema/src/text.ts`), never from the runtime. Each runtime's copy of CLDR gives its own figures: Node 22 gave COP, HUF, IDR and PKR no decimals and RSD two, where Chromium 141 gave the reverse, so a close's line from the API said "COP 100,000, HUF 123,456 and RSD 50.00" while the same close's details on Reports said "COP 1,000.00, HUF 1,234.56 and RSD 5,000", and another browser could say something else again. Both now say COP 1,000.00, HUF 1,234.56 and RSD 50.00 whatever the browser: the console tells Intl how many decimals to show rather than asking it. A code ISO 4217 gives no minor unit, or does not list, is written in its smallest unit, in the same words on both sides ("100,000 in the smallest unit of XAU"), rather than with decimals guessed for it. An exception raised for such money holds its minor units too, and names its currency (`data.currency`), so the Exceptions row, the resolve dialog, the case page and the customer's history show it in that currency (USD 1,000.00), where they showed it as naira (₦1,000.00); an exception an earlier build raised without the currency gains it at the next reconciliation. Import batches shows each converted amount in its row's currency, as the quick import's preview does, and neither it nor Sources adds currencies into one total: a source or imported total sums the naira rows and lists each other currency beside it ("₦15.00, JPY 1,000 (1 row) and USD 10.00 (1 row)"), as a close lists money in another currency. A batch committed by an earlier build keeps the totals it was committed with, which may add rows in other currencies, and Sources and the completeness panel say so rather than claim that no total adds them. An instalment, a mandate or an attempt imported with a currency column keeps that column as detail and its amount in kobo, so the customer page shows it as naira: only a kind with a currency field (a payment and its evidence, a settlement batch, an exception) is shown in the currency it names (`formatRecordMoney`).

**Under other settings.** With the browser set to German in New York, and to American English in Los Angeles, the console showed "19 Sept 2026, 07:00 WAT (daily)" for the next close and "18 Sept 2026, 13:50 WAT" on the audit log, exactly as it did set to Nigerian English in Lagos, while the device's own rendering of one instant was "18.9.2026, 18:30:00" in the first and "9/18/2026, 3:30:00 PM" in the second.

**The typefaces.** Plus Jakarta Sans, the console's face, carries ẹ, ọ, ṣ, Ṣ, the combining tone marks and the naira sign in the subsets Google Fonts serves, which the console now serves itself (checked in the font files themselves); the arrow and "at least" signs in two report chips fall to the system font. Spline Sans Mono, the face for references and amounts, has no naira sign, so the ₦ before an amount is drawn by the device's monospace face beside Spline's digits: legible, one glyph, and left as it is; the cure would be a mono face that has the sign, or the sans with tabular figures for amounts, which is a typographic decision rather than a correctness one. In the PDF the embedded face draws every letter.

**What we left out.** No translation layer: the product has one language, its strings live in the components, and a message catalogue for a second language would be a project of its own; the API's messages are English prose without codes for the same reason. No right-to-left layout: the styles say left and right rather than start and end in about 270 places, a mechanical change (ml to ms, pr to pe, text-right to text-end, and so on) for the day a right-to-left market is in scope, not before. The uppercase labels transform English only, where the transform is safe. The identifiers the API orders with `localeCompare` are lowercase hexadecimal and ISO timestamps, on which every collation agrees.


## What we left out, on purpose

- No hero animation, video, carousel, chat widget or cookie banner. No cookie is set until the sandbox is opened, and that cookie is the sandbox session itself.
- No customer testimonials, customer logos or unmeasured performance claims. The recovery uplift is not on the page because Test 2 has not been proven; every number shown carries its basis, and the hero artwork is labelled as an illustration with synthetic figures.
- No "fully compliant", "leading" or "seamless". Regulators are named specifically and the wording is "written to".
- No lead-capture form. The ways in are the sandbox and sign-in.
- No changes to console or sign-in styling in the landing template refresh. Its scoped palette keeps the shared brand and controls while allowing the public page's warmer surfaces and decorative artwork.

## Operational follow-up

The next pass makes a queue row a starting point for work. Its next action opens the specific mandate, customer event or instalment payment review, identifies the selected record and returns to the original collection filters and row. A return address is accepted only for the collections route and the same lender. Moving between lists should not require remembering a reference or rebuilding a queue (Nielsen 3 and 6).

Long lists offer 25, 50 or 100 rows per page. Customers and Audit log request only that page and wait for a 300 ms pause before searching. Exceptions, Mandates and Collections request server-filtered priority pages of 25, 50 or 100 rows. Search matches customer names and record references without case or accent sensitivity. Page, size and filters are lender-bound URL state, so browser Back and customer return links restore the queue. Saved views store a queue's status, owner and type per person, lender and queue in this browser, never its search text or result rows, and opening one clears the current search: a search can hold a customer's name or reference, which has no place in browser storage. Views an earlier build saved with their search text are removed when a queue with saved views is opened. Keeping views on the server, and searches out of page addresses, waits for real data. Reconciliation requests six separate server-paged queues with complete totals and only the related records needed by each page. The allocation picker fetches a searchable page of instalments when opened and searches after the same pause; typing never adds a history entry, so Back leaves the page rather than stepping back through letters. It asks only for instalments that can take a payment (the list's `allocatable`), and only those a manual allocation of the payment it allocates accepts (`paymentId`: the payer's, or, when the evidence names an instalment but no payer, that instalment's customer's), so its pager counts exactly the choices it offers and none it offers is refused: it used to filter a page in the browser and count everything the server returned, so a search could promise "1–1 of 1 instalment choices" and offer none, and a payment whose evidence named an instalment but no payer was offered every customer's instalments, most of which the allocation then refused. It says that paid, cancelled, closed and disputed instalments are not listed, and when none qualifies it says so in one plain line instead of a pager (Nielsen 1 and 9). The mandate form's customer picker asks for one searchable page of customers in the same way, and each new mandate starts with an empty search and no customer chosen, after Cancel or after a mandate is created; before, the next Add mandate kept the last search. Precision review pages the seeded previous-month sample, including superseded allocations already reviewed as wrong. Reports request compact, server-filtered daily-close summaries; opening a close fetches its full evidence. A close's evidence lists money held in another currency beside its naira, each currency with its code and how many payments hold it, never added to the naira ("Unallocated at close: 1 · ₦0.00 and USD 1,000.00 (1 payment) · 1 older than 24 hours", with the count the close recorded), so a lone USD payment is never shown as nothing waiting; before, the evidence showed its naira and nothing else (`lib/currencies.ts`). The comparison between the first and latest closes in a range shows the unmatched value in naira only and says so, because the close list carries only that; each close's details list the rest. The billing statement's receipts by payment method list receipts in another currency beside a method's naira value in the same way. Inclusive WAT date comparisons use the first and latest closes across the entire selected range, independent of the visible page. Current operational and billing measures remain unchanged. The narrower requests reduce network payloads; production capacity still requires deployment-specific measurement.

Before a mandate action, the confirmation names the customer and mandate, shows the current and resulting state and explains consequences and recovery. Suspension can be followed by Resume, but cancelled attempts stay cancelled; cancellation requires a new mandate with fresh consent if collection is to resume. Reissue starts with blank consent evidence and offers the new record after completion. These distinctions prevent a generic confirmation from implying that every change can be undone (Nielsen 5 and 9).

The case form offers only what the service accepts. A case someone holds can be changed only by that person or an Admin, so anyone else sees the form locked with the reason; a case goes only to someone on the lender's case list, which the assignee menu lists, and a former assignee no longer on it must hand the case over; claiming assigns the case to the person claiming it. The next action and the note need at least 3 characters and the follow-up a future time, and each problem is named at its field before anything is sent (Nielsen 5 and 9). The link to resolve the exception opens Exceptions on that exception alone, focused, whatever its status, with a way back to the queue.

Two destructive changes were decided on together. Revoking a staff member on Team and access takes one more step after its reason: a confirmation names the person, says that they lose access to the workspace and every lender in it at their next request, that their lender access and any waiting invitation are removed, and that only a new invitation brings them back, and repeats the reason the access history will record; Keep access closes it and returns focus to Save access change, and nothing is sent until Revoke access, after which focus goes to the member's message that says what the revocation did. The emergency stop on Settings stays one step after its reason, because it is the control that must work fastest when something goes wrong, and turning it off again is one step too (Nielsen 3 and 5; Shneiderman: permit easy reversal, offer error prevention).

On Team and access a member's card lasts as long as the membership, and its forms start again from each version of it, so after any change, made there or by another administrator, they show the membership as it now stands; what a change made on the card did, and a change whose answer was lost, stay on the card. Before, the card was rebuilt with each version, so an applied revocation, suspension, role change or lender access change lost its confirmation, and only a change waiting for a second administrator kept its message. Someone who is not an administrator sees only the colleagues who share one of their lenders, with only those lenders, so a colleague's row counts them as lenders you share ("1 lender you share") rather than as the colleague's permitted lenders; the viewer's own row, and every row an administrator sees, still counts the permitted lenders (Nielsen 1 and 2).

The emergency controls on Settings say what a stop request did on the page, in the service's words ("Lender emergency stop is off. No collection instruction was sent."), rather than in a notice that goes away. While another administrator's request to lift the stop waits, its box offers Approve turning it off and Keep the stop on; if a stop request's answer is lost there, the main button offers to retry the original request, as it does without a waiting request, and nothing that could reverse the lost request is offered until its outcome is known: Approve turning it off waits after a lost Keep the stop on, and Keep the stop on and the main button wait after a lost approval. A lost approval's notice, with its retry, stays below the box, so a refetch that shows the request settled, as the approval may have settled it, no longer takes the only way to recover it with the box. Before, a lost Keep the stop on left no retry and the approval enabled (Nielsen 1, 3 and 5; Shneiderman: permit easy reversal).

An approved retention run now carries itself on. Execute approved run (now Start deletion) used to delete one source and stop, so a run of a hundred sources took a hundred clicks, each of which looked like a finished step. The page now asks again until the run completes, a source is blocked or a deletion cannot be confirmed. Meanwhile the page's status area says how many sources are removed, with a progress bar and Stop, which takes the focus while Start deletion waits, disabled; Stop ends the run after the current request, because a deletion already sent cannot be recalled. When the run stops, the status area says why instead (the blocked source and its receipt's reason, a request the service refused, in its words, or a request that failed or whose answer was lost, which may have removed more and which Check original request in the notice above resolves) and how many sources were removed so far, and reading continues from it; before, a failed or lost request left the status area empty and focus on the page body. Continue deletion carries the run on. Leaving the page while a run is going asks first (Nielsen 1, 3 and 5; Shneiderman: informative feedback and user control).

An import no longer loses a column quietly. A customer file with its names in `full_name` used to pass the check as valid while its mapping showed Skip column, and committing it named every customer after their reference. The mapping now suggests a field for a header it recognises (`full_name` as Full name) and shows it as an unsaved change to save and check; the check says, in a warning box above the rows, when a name or reference will come from a fallback while a column is unused, and which column looks like the missing field; and committing a batch, or importing sample data, whose check warns asks first, naming the fallbacks, with Review the mapping as the way back (Nielsen 1, 5 and 9; Shneiderman: prevent errors).

Returning collection ownership states its consequences the same way before it is confirmed: how many instalments Valo Pay collects now, how many scheduled attempts it cancels and the fallback owner the cutover contract names, read afresh when the dialog opens and chosen as the service chooses it, and that the emergency stop turns on for the lender and must be turned off separately. The old dialog said only that the action could not collect money or message a customer, and the result was thrown away. Now the result is announced with the service's own message and counts, and the page shows the stop on at once (Nielsen 1, 5 and 9).

Policy and template reviews place the version, available previous values and a synthetic customer-message example beside the decision. Temporary customer-history errors offer retry and are distinct from a confirmed missing customer. ## Backlog decisions · September 2026

The owner's decisions on the usability backlog (`docs/usability/audit.md`) changed what a person sees on several pages; the reasoning is recorded here, and what each changed in the first section of `docs/BUILD_STATUS.md`.

Recovery stays manual, so the console says where to recover. Until real data is admitted, a reloaded form does not look up its own request; instead, every notice of a change whose retry a reload would lose, the export control's, the quick import's and those of a daily close, a reconciliation run and an audit log check among them, sends the person to the one place that keeps it. Each Operations entry says what it asked in words ("Change a record", "Import a checked batch"), which record it names and a few short fields such as a decision or a format, never a name, reference, amount or file; a pending or cancelled entry links to that record, and a completed one to its saved result for every kind a page shows, an export or a retention run by its ID, since Saved exports otherwise selects the newest export and Data retention lists only the ten newest runs. Before, an entry was named only by its route or action, and Open saved result reached four kinds, so a person had to remember what they had sent (Nielsen 1, 6 and 9: visibility, recognition rather than recall, recovery). The navigation's count makes a waiting request visible from any page.

An export whose file was removed says what happened and what remains, rather than only that it has expired: when it was removed and by which retention run, the checksum of the removed file, or for an export that never finished that it has none, and that the deletion receipt is saved with that run. An administrator opens the run from the card (Open the deletion record), which Data retention shows from its address (`/lifecycle?run=<id>`), since its own list holds only the latest ten runs and every preview counts as one; anyone else is told that an administrator sees that link on the same export. Saved exports filters by File deleted, the words the rows already use, so Ready to download means a file that can still be downloaded. A ready export shows no date by which it will go: a hold or a link to an open case can keep a file past the retention period, and a date the service cannot keep would teach the wrong thing (Nielsen 1, 2 and 9; Norman: knowledge in the world).

A quick import names its rows as an import batch does. The Row ID column is chosen from the file's header before the first check, with a column headed as a source row ID suggested, and the next step says so while it is missing, or asks for a header row when the file's header cannot be read; a reference chosen as the row ID stays mapped as the reference. The disclosure says how a row is recognised: skipped when its row ID came before with the same data, refused when the data or the reference differs. Row errors speak the operator's language: each names the column by the shared label and the operator's own header where they differ, and says what to enter, with every rule a row fails listed at once; the record API's own words stay in the errors CSV's Technical detail column, so nothing is hidden from someone who needs them. Both import screens open their results on the rows to fix, with the same download and Edit CSV, and say "skipped as already imported". One set of labels names each field on the quick import, Import batches, the sample files, the customer form and the row errors (Nielsen 2, 4, 5 and 9; Shneiderman: offer error prevention and simple error handling).

Saved views keep filters only (above, under Operational follow-up), and team, access and lender set-up ask before they are left while a change is unconfirmed (above, under Errors).

## Permission changes and accounting review recovery

Cash Desk offers Refresh accounting review when a prepared ERP draft has lost its permission or source binding. Preparing the draft again is an explicit maker action; it does not silently restore Finance approval. Review and download controls are withheld until current permissions, accounting period, receipts and reviewed contents agree. Payroll approvals similarly expire when their permission changes. A saved VAT schedule or forecast whose permission or evidence changed stays listed with its date and author, without its figures or download, and says to save it again under the current permission. When a connected action's retry is refused because its saved answer is withheld, the page shows the service's words and is free again at once, rather than holding the request until it is discarded. This makes the next recovery step visible and prevents an old file from appearing current (Nielsen 1, 3, 5 and 9).
